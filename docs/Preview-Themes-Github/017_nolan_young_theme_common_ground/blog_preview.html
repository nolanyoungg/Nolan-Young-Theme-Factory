01 / LightStudio notes · 2 min read

Before adding glass,
follow the light.

A room can have a large window and still feel dim. The depth of the plan, the direction it faces, and the surfaces opposite the opening all influence how light travels. Start by noticing where you actually spend time, rather than assuming that more glazing is always the answer.

Watch an ordinary day

Make a simple plan and mark the pools of light in the morning, at midday, and in the late afternoon. Repeat in different seasons if you can. Note where you turn on a lamp, where glare makes a screen difficult to read, and which rooms become uncomfortable in strong sun.

Think about the path, not just the opening

A lighter ceiling or a wider internal opening may let borrowed light reach the center of a home. Moving a tall cupboard can change the way a room feels without changing a wall. Where new glazing makes sense, consider shade, privacy, views, and heat together.

A useful first step

Photograph the same room from the same position at three times of day. Bring those observations into your first design conversation.

02 / MaterialsStudio notes · 2 min read

Choose for the years
after the photograph.

A material sample is usually small, clean, and perfectly lit. Daily life is less controlled. Shoes bring in grit, chairs move, and water gathers near a sink. A useful material choice accounts for those ordinary moments, as well as the impression on the first day.

Ask how it will be maintained

Can the surface be cleaned with readily available products? Can a damaged section be replaced without removing the whole assembly? Some finishes invite local repair; others rely on an unbroken coating. Neither is automatically right, but the upkeep should fit the way you want to live.

Put the samples together

Look at the floor beside the joinery, the wall finish beside the window frame. View them in the actual room and under evening light. A restrained palette often allows texture and proportion to do more of the work, and can make future repairs easier to integrate.

A useful first step

Ask for maintenance information with each sample. Compare the work needed over time, not only the purchase price.

03 / ReuseStudio notes · 2 min read

Begin with
what remains.

The first design move in a renovation can be a list of things to keep. That list might include the structure, but also an old door, a useful shelf, or the way two rooms connect. Looking carefully helps separate a real problem from something that simply looks unfamiliar.

Make a keep, adapt, replace inventory

Walk through the building before selecting new finishes. Record each element, its condition, and why it matters. A door that no longer suits one opening may work elsewhere. Sound joinery might need a new layout rather than complete replacement. Condition and suitability should be assessed before committing to reuse.

Leave room for discovery

Existing buildings rarely reveal everything at once. Allow time for surveys and specialist advice, and keep a contingency for what may be uncovered. Reuse can reduce unnecessary replacement, but careful removal, storage, and adaptation still require planning and a realistic budget.

A useful first step

Choose five things you would miss if they disappeared. That small list can be the beginning of a more personal renovation brief.

Good places begin with a conversation.

A room to rethink. A small plot. An idea for your neighborhood.
